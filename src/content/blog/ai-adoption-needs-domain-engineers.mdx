# AI Adoption Needs Domain Engineers

Buying an AI tool is easy.

Making it useful is harder.

The hard part is not always the model.

The hard part is the work around the model:

```txt
Which task should AI handle?
Which data can it see?
Who reviews the output?
What happens when it is wrong?
How does it fit the existing workflow?
How do we measure whether it helped?
```

That is not just prompting.

That is implementation.

And implementation needs people who understand both the domain and the technology.

AI adoption needs domain engineers.

## The Fresh Signal

On September 15, 2026, [Business Insider reported](https://www.businessinsider.com/openai-legal-engineers-law-firms-telon-2026-9) that OpenAI is working with legal-engineering startup Telon to help law firms adopt AI tools more effectively.

The article says Telon works with major firms to build AI workflows, train lawyers, and identify use cases where AI can actually save time instead of simply adding another tool.

[Business Insider also reported](https://www.businessinsider.com/openai-law-firm-paul-weiss-harvey-legal-ai-2026-9) on OpenAI's legal-sector push through partnerships involving firms such as Paul Weiss and tools such as Harvey.

The specific companies matter less than the pattern:

```txt
AI adoption is moving from tool access
to workflow translation
```

This is not only a law-firm story.

It is the story every serious industry will run into.

## The Tool Is Not The Transformation

A company can buy access to a powerful model and still get very little value.

People may use it for:

- email polishing
- generic summaries
- occasional brainstorming
- quick explanations
- small drafting tasks

That is useful.

But it is not transformation.

Real transformation happens when AI is embedded into a workflow:

- intake
- research
- drafting
- review
- approval
- filing
- reporting
- monitoring
- follow-up

That takes design.

It takes domain knowledge.

It takes trust.

It takes iteration.

It takes someone who understands how the work actually happens, not how it looks in a slide deck.

## Why Law Is A Good Example

Law is a perfect example because it is full of text.

Contracts.

Briefs.

Memos.

Emails.

Regulations.

Case law.

Policies.

Due diligence documents.

AI is obviously useful there.

But law is also full of risk.

Confidentiality matters.

Accuracy matters.

Privilege matters.

Jurisdiction matters.

Source quality matters.

Review matters.

Accountability matters.

So a law firm cannot simply say:

```txt
use the AI
```

It has to ask:

```txt
use it where, with what data, under whose supervision, and with what review standard?
```

That is where legal engineers become important.

## What A Domain Engineer Does

A domain engineer is not just a prompt writer.

They are a translator between real work and AI systems.

They understand:

- the professional workflow
- the failure modes
- the review culture
- the data boundaries
- the compliance rules
- the incentives
- the language of the domain
- the tooling environment
- the business outcome

Then they turn that into:

- workflow maps
- prompt patterns
- templates
- retrieval rules
- tool integrations
- approval gates
- evaluation sets
- training material
- usage policies
- metrics
- feedback loops

This is why "AI adoption" often needs a new kind of operator.

Not pure engineer.

Not pure consultant.

Not pure domain expert.

A hybrid.

Someone who can sit with the work long enough to make the tool fit reality.

## Generic AI Training Is Not Enough

Many organizations start with generic AI training:

```txt
Here is how to write better prompts.
Here are ten use cases.
Here are the risks.
Go experiment.
```

That is fine as a first step.

But it fades quickly.

People return to their real work.

They hit messy documents.

They hit approval processes.

They hit unclear data rules.

They hit clients who expect precision.

They hit tools that do not connect.

They hit outputs that are 80 percent good but require 90 percent of the old review time.

Generic training does not solve that.

Domain engineering does.

It asks:

```txt
What exact work should change?
```

Then it builds the change.

## The Hidden Work Of Adoption

Good AI adoption involves a lot of unglamorous work.

For example:

```txt
Identify a repeatable task.
Collect representative examples.
Define what good output looks like.
Decide what data is allowed.
Create a review checklist.
Build prompts and templates.
Test against real cases.
Measure time saved and error rate.
Train users on the workflow.
Log failures.
Improve the workflow.
Decide when not to use AI.
```

That is not a weekend workshop.

That is product development inside a profession.

The AI model is part of it.

But the model is not the whole thing.

## Adoption Fails When It Ignores Review

In serious fields, AI output is rarely "done."

It is usually:

```txt
draft
summary
candidate answer
first pass
triage result
recommendation
```

The review loop matters.

Who checks the work?

What are they checking for?

What sources are required?

What uncertainty must be shown?

Which outputs can go directly to clients or customers?

Which require senior approval?

Which should never be automated?

This connects to [AI Interfaces Still Need Buttons](/blog/ai-interfaces-still-need-buttons).

Serious AI workflows need previews, approvals, undo, logs, and visible state.

In professional settings, the interface must support review.

Otherwise AI becomes a fancy way to create unreviewed risk.

## Domain Engineers Build Evaluations

AI evaluation is not only a lab benchmark.

Every organization adopting AI needs local evaluations.

A law firm might test:

- citation accuracy
- issue spotting
- contract clause extraction
- privilege risk
- jurisdiction-specific reasoning
- hallucinated cases
- redline quality
- confidentiality boundaries

A hospital might test:

- summarization accuracy
- triage safety
- handoff quality
- missing allergy flags
- patient privacy

A software team might test:

- patch correctness
- test coverage
- security regressions
- style consistency
- ability to explain changes

Generic benchmarks cannot answer all of that.

Domain engineers create the local tests that matter.

This connects to [What AI Benchmarks Mean](/blog/what-ai-benchmarks-mean).

Benchmarks are useful, but the real question is:

```txt
does this model work for this workflow, under these constraints?
```

## Adoption Also Needs Policy

Every useful AI workflow has a policy surface.

People need to know:

- what data can be uploaded
- what data cannot be uploaded
- when AI use must be disclosed
- what outputs require human review
- what tools are approved
- how outputs are stored
- how mistakes are reported
- who owns the workflow
- how the workflow is retired

This is why [Agent Rules Should Live Outside The Prompt](/blog/agent-rules-should-live-outside-the-prompt).

The rules cannot live only in a training slide or system prompt.

They need to become workflow controls.

Domain engineers help translate policy into working practice.

## The Same Pattern Applies Everywhere

Legal work is only one example.

The same pattern applies to:

- healthcare
- finance
- education
- insurance
- accounting
- government services
- software engineering
- customer support
- procurement
- manufacturing
- cybersecurity

Every field has its own:

- vocabulary
- documents
- workflows
- risks
- approval habits
- quality standards
- regulatory constraints

That means every serious AI deployment needs some local adaptation.

The closer the work is to real consequences, the more domain engineering matters.

## The Job Title May Change

Maybe the title is not "domain engineer."

It might be:

- legal engineer
- AI workflow architect
- clinical AI specialist
- AI operations lead
- automation product manager
- knowledge engineer
- AI transformation lead
- practice technologist

The title matters less than the function.

The function is:

```txt
turn AI capability into safe, useful, measurable work
```

That is a job.

And it is becoming more important.

## The Takeaway

AI adoption is entering the implementation phase.

The question is no longer only:

```txt
Which model should we buy?
```

The better question is:

```txt
Who will translate this model into our work?
```

That translation requires domain engineers.

People who understand the profession.

People who understand AI enough to shape workflows.

People who can build review loops, policies, prompts, integrations, and evaluations.

Tools do not adopt themselves.

The real value comes from the bridge between technology and domain judgment.
