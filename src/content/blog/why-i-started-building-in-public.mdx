# Why I Started Building in Public

A few years ago, most of my work lived only on my computer.

Projects stayed unfinished.

Experiments were never shared.

Ideas remained buried in notebooks, folders, and random local branches.

Like many developers, I used to think I had to build something perfect before showing it to the world.

I was wrong.

Over time, I realized that growth happens much faster when you build in public.

Not because your work is perfect, but because it is not.

---

## What Building in Public Means

Building in public means sharing your journey openly.

It can look like:

* Writing blog posts.
* Publishing projects on GitHub.
* Sharing progress on LinkedIn.
* Documenting experiments.
* Talking about failures as well as successes.
* Showing what you are learning while you are still learning it.

Instead of only showing finished products, you show the process.

And that process is often more valuable than the finished thing.

---

## My Journey

Over the past few years, I have worked on many different things:

* Laravel and React applications.
* Odoo custom modules.
* Flutter projects.
* Local AI experiments using Ollama and LM Studio.
* AI agents like OpenClaw, Hermes, and Odysseus.
* Personal finance applications.
* Booking systems.
* My portfolio website and blog.

Not every project became successful.

Some never made it beyond the prototype stage.

Some broke repeatedly.

Some taught me lessons that no tutorial ever could.

And that is exactly why sharing them matters.

---

## Blogging Changed How I Learn

When I started writing technical blogs, I noticed something interesting.

Explaining a concept forces you to understand it more clearly.

Writing about AI quantization made me study how model precision works.

Writing about local LLMs helped me understand deployment and optimization better.

Writing about AI agents pushed me to compare tools critically instead of only using them casually.

In many ways, blogging became a form of learning.

The internet often treats writing as teaching.

For me, it has often been the opposite.

Writing is learning.

---

## GitHub Is More Than a Code Repository

For me, GitHub became a timeline of growth.

When I look back at older repositories, I do not just see code.

I see:

* Bugs I struggled with.
* Design decisions I later changed.
* Technologies I learned.
* Problems I eventually solved.
* Ideas that became better after several attempts.

Every commit tells a small story.

Many developers hesitate to publish projects because they think their code is not good enough.

But growth is visible only when there is history.

An imperfect public repository is often more valuable than a perfect project that nobody can see.

---

## Learning Openly Creates Unexpected Opportunities

One thing I did not expect was how many conversations started because I shared my work.

A blog post can lead to a discussion.

A GitHub repository can attract feedback.

A LinkedIn post can start a technical conversation.

Sometimes people reach out with suggestions.

Sometimes they ask questions.

Sometimes they introduce completely new ideas.

Opportunities often come from visibility.

People cannot discover work that remains hidden.

---

## Public Work Creates Accountability

Building in public also creates a subtle form of accountability.

When I publish a project or write about a goal, I feel more motivated to continue working on it.

This has helped me maintain:

* Personal projects.
* Technical blogs.
* Continuous learning.
* Long-term experiments.

Progress does not have to be fast.

But public progress tends to become more consistent.

---

## Not Everything Needs to Succeed

One of the biggest lessons I have learned is this:

```text
Not every project needs to become a startup.
```

Some projects exist purely to learn.

Some exist to explore curiosity.

Some exist to fail.

And that is okay.

A failed project that teaches you something is rarely a waste of time.

In technology, experiments compound.

Knowledge from one project often becomes useful years later in a completely different context.

---

## Why I Continue Building in Public

Today, I continue sharing blogs, projects, and experiments.

Not because I know everything, but because I am still learning.

The internet often rewards finished products.

But real growth usually happens behind the scenes.

Building in public brings those scenes into the open.

That might be the most valuable part of all.

You do not need to be an expert to share your journey.

You only need to be one step ahead of where you were yesterday.

---

## Final Thoughts

The best time to start building in public is not after becoming an expert.

It is now.

Publish the project.

Write the blog.

Push the commit.

Share the experiment.

Years later, you may realize you were not just building software.

You were building a career.
