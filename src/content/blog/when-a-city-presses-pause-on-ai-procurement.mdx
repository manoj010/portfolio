# When a City Presses Pause on AI Procurement

When a government adopts an AI system, it is not only choosing software. It may be choosing how residents' information is processed, how public workers make decisions, and what happens when an automated recommendation is wrong.

That is the reasoning behind a proposal from Portland City Council President Jamie Dunphy: temporarily pause new city procurement and implementation of AI while the city establishes working privacy oversight and writes clearer rules. The proposal is scheduled for council consideration in December. **It has not been adopted**, so Portland has not yet enacted a moratorium. ([Portland's announcement](https://www.portland.gov/council/districts/1/jamie-dunphy/news/2026/11/17/council-president-dunphy-proposes-temporary-halt); [draft ordinance](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/ordinance/ai-moratorium))

## This Is About Buying, Not Banning AI Research

The draft is aimed at the City's own purchases and deployments. It would cover more than a conventional paid contract: the text includes upgrades that add AI features, pilots, trials, exchanges, and even no-cost or giveaway arrangements. Existing contracts could generally continue through their current term, but renewing them or expanding their AI functionality would require a Council exemption.

The proposal also contains exceptions. Basic AI that is incidental to software already in use, such as spell-check or spam filtering, is treated differently from a system that analyzes personal data or influences a consequential decision. Cybersecurity uses are exempt. A new pilot could be considered by Council if it demonstrates a specific need and public benefit, has limited risk and clear disclosure, costs less than $250,000, lasts no more than 12 months, and does not use city data to train or improve a vendor's model. Those are provisions in a **proposed** ordinance, not rules currently in force. ([Draft ordinance and Exhibit A](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/ordinance/ai-moratorium))

That distinction matters. A procurement pause is not the same thing as stopping private AI development, banning all software that contains a model, or suspending every existing city use. The proposal is a buyer's tool: it would temporarily limit what the city can add while it builds a process for judging future purchases.

## The Capacity Problem Comes First

The city's stated reason is unusually concrete. Portland created a Data and Privacy Office earlier this year, but the office is not yet operational and cannot review technology contracts. The proposal argues that without this capacity and clearer policy, the city cannot reliably assess privacy, security, civil-rights, public-records, and data-governance risks before procuring or piloting AI systems.

That reframes the debate from “Is AI good or bad?” to a more answerable question: **Does the organization have the people, authority, and information needed to evaluate what it is buying?** A city cannot meaningfully compare a vendor's promises with its own risk tolerance if it does not know what tools it already has, which departments use them, or what data flows into them.

So the draft requires a citywide inventory of AI in use, approved, awaiting approval, or under contract. It calls for an initial report within 60 days of adoption and updates at least quarterly. The inventory would be a practical starting point: before writing rules for “AI,” the Council and the public need a picture of where the technology is already embedded.

## A Pause Can Create Accountability, But It Has A Cost

The strongest case for a temporary pause is that it makes room to create minimum safeguards before new tools become routine. Procurement is one of the few points where a government can ask vendors concrete questions about data handling, model training, testing, retention, audit access, and accountability. Once a service is integrated into a workflow, changing providers or rolling it back can be much harder.

But a pause is not a substitute for permanent governance. It can delay tools that might help staff or residents, and it may be difficult to distinguish an incidental AI feature from a substantial new capability inside an existing product. The draft tries to draw those lines with “minimal risk” criteria and a Council-approved pilot process, but the definitions will need careful administration. Too broad, and useful low-risk applications get stuck. Too narrow, and the review gate becomes easy to route around.

The city also needs to define what happens after the pause. The draft would expire at the end of 2027 unless extended, with an option to end sooner once the Data and Privacy Office is operational and the Council has adopted AI-use legislation. That makes the pause a bridge to a governance system, not a policy endpoint.

## Public Buyers Can Set The Terms

Private companies often frame AI adoption as an internal efficiency decision. Governments have another obligation: they must be able to explain how a tool affects the people who rely on public services, including people who never chose to interact with it.

Portland's proposal is worth watching because it treats procurement as a governance lever. The immediate test is not whether a city can stop technology from advancing. It is whether a public institution can inventory its current systems, staff a credible review office, set measurable pilot criteria, and make vendors answer for how their products use public data.

If the Council adopts the measure, the pause will only be useful if that work actually happens. A temporary stop without a better process just delays the same decision. A pause paired with inventory, expertise, clear exceptions, and enforceable contract terms could make the next decision more informed and easier for residents to scrutinize.
