# AI Safety Pacts Need Clear Rules

AI safety has a coordination problem.

If frontier labs race too hard, they may cut corners.

If they coordinate too quietly, they may protect themselves.

That is the uncomfortable tension.

People want AI companies to slow down when systems become risky.

That is reasonable.

But people also do not want a handful of powerful companies deciding the future of AI behind closed doors.

That is also reasonable.

So the question is not:

```txt
Should AI labs coordinate?
```

The better question is:

```txt
What rules make coordination trustworthy?
```

AI safety pacts need clear rules.

## The Fresh Signal

On September 14, 2026, [The Verge reported](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/995186/is-big-techs-ai-slowdown-a-safety-pact-or-a-cartel) on the tension between voluntary AI slowdown agreements and antitrust concerns.

The core issue is easy to understand.

If major AI companies agree to pause or slow deployment for safety reasons, that could be responsible.

But if the same agreement reduces competition, blocks smaller players, or lets incumbents control the market, it starts to look less like safety and more like cartel behavior.

This is where AI governance gets tricky.

[NIST's AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) treats AI risk management as a structured process involving governance, mapping, measurement, and management.

The [EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/the-act/) also shows a different path: instead of relying only on voluntary promises, it creates legal obligations around risk categories, transparency, and compliance.

The lesson is not that one framework solves everything.

The lesson is:

```txt
trustworthy safety needs structure
```

## Coordination Can Be Good

There are real reasons AI companies may need to coordinate.

Frontier models can create shared risks:

- cyber misuse
- biological misuse
- automated persuasion
- fraud
- model extraction
- synthetic media abuse
- unsafe agent behavior
- dangerous tool access
- evaluation gaming

No single company can manage all of that alone.

If every lab knows a certain capability threshold is dangerous, it may be better for labs to agree on safety testing before release.

That kind of coordination can prevent a race to the bottom.

The same thing happens in other industries.

Aviation has safety standards.

Medicine has clinical trials.

Finance has reporting obligations.

Cybersecurity has disclosure norms.

Coordination is not automatically bad.

But it has to be designed carefully.

## Coordination Can Also Be Self-Serving

The problem is that safety language can hide business incentives.

A big company might say:

```txt
we need strict safety rules
```

and mean:

```txt
we need standards only companies our size can afford
```

Another company might say:

```txt
we should slow down frontier deployment
```

and mean:

```txt
we are already ahead, so freezing the race helps us
```

A group of companies might say:

```txt
we are coordinating responsibly
```

and still create barriers that make it harder for new entrants, open-source labs, academic groups, or smaller startups to compete.

This is why safety pacts need sunlight.

Not every detail can be public.

Some security-sensitive information should stay protected.

But the criteria, process, and accountability should be visible enough to evaluate.

## The Difference Between A Pact And A Cartel

A safety pact should reduce risk.

A cartel reduces competition.

The uncomfortable part is that the same behavior can look similar from the outside:

```txt
companies agree not to release something
```

The difference depends on details.

A credible safety pact has:

- public goals
- defined risk thresholds
- independent review
- narrow scope
- time limits
- incident reporting
- appeal paths
- regulator visibility
- competition safeguards

A suspicious pact has:

- vague danger claims
- closed membership
- no audit process
- no timeline
- unclear release criteria
- private market coordination
- standards that only incumbents can meet
- no way for outsiders to challenge decisions

That is the line.

Safety coordination should be inspectable.

Market coordination should not hide inside safety language.

## Safety Thresholds Must Be Specific

If a company says:

```txt
we will pause if the model is too dangerous
```

that sounds responsible.

It is also too vague.

Too dangerous how?

For whom?

Under what test?

Compared to what baseline?

With which mitigations?

For how long?

A useful safety pact should define thresholds.

For example:

```txt
The model cannot be released broadly if it enables a non-expert to complete a high-risk cyber task above a defined success rate.
```

Or:

```txt
The model cannot get autonomous tool access until it passes third-party evaluations for prompt injection resistance and action-boundary compliance.
```

Or:

```txt
The model must not be deployed in high-impact domains without documented human oversight, logging, and rollback paths.
```

The exact thresholds will be debated.

Good.

Debate is better than mystical safety vibes.

## Independent Audits Matter

If the companies building frontier models are also the only ones judging whether those models are safe, trust will stay weak.

That does not mean every model detail should be exposed publicly.

But there should be independent review.

Audits could examine:

- evaluation design
- dangerous-capability testing
- deployment controls
- incident history
- data governance
- cybersecurity posture
- misuse monitoring
- rollback procedures
- red-team results at an appropriate disclosure level

The point is not to slow everything forever.

The point is to avoid self-certified safety becoming a marketing label.

This connects to [AI Incidents Need Precise Language](/blog/ai-incidents-need-precise-language).

If incidents and evaluations are described vaguely, nobody can learn from them.

## Timelines Prevent Permanent Fog

A voluntary pause should not be indefinite without explanation.

If a lab delays release, it should be able to say:

```txt
what triggered the delay
what work is being done
what criteria would allow release
when the decision will be reviewed
who is involved in the review
```

Without timelines, safety pauses become fog.

Fog helps nobody.

Users do not understand what is happening.

Researchers cannot evaluate the standard.

Smaller labs cannot plan.

Regulators cannot tell whether the process is real.

Competitors cannot tell whether the pact is safety work or market control.

Timelines create pressure to explain.

That pressure is useful.

## Open Source Needs A Seat

AI governance debates often center on the biggest labs.

That is understandable.

They train the largest models.

They have the biggest deployment platforms.

They create many of the frontier risks.

But open-source and open-weight communities matter too.

If safety pacts are designed only by incumbents, they may accidentally or intentionally create rules that smaller projects cannot satisfy.

For example:

- audit fees that only giant companies can afford
- reporting systems built for enterprise legal teams
- compute thresholds that miss smaller but risky models
- licensing assumptions that do not fit open ecosystems
- access rules that block independent research

This is why I wrote [Open Source Needs AI Rules, Not Panic](/blog/open-source-needs-ai-rules-not-panic).

Good rules should be serious without becoming an incumbent protection system.

## Regulators Should Watch The Boundary

Governments have a hard job here.

They should not punish genuine safety coordination.

They also should not give major companies a blank check to coordinate privately.

The healthiest approach is probably:

```txt
allow narrow, documented safety coordination
require transparency around criteria and process
watch for anti-competitive behavior
protect independent research
create public-interest audit channels
```

That is harder than slogans.

But AI governance is now too important for slogans.

If companies coordinate on safety, regulators should ask:

- Who is included?
- Who is excluded?
- What exactly is being delayed?
- Which risks are being addressed?
- What evidence supports the decision?
- Who audits the process?
- What market effects follow?
- Can smaller labs comply?
- Is there a public-interest reason?

These questions do not block safety.

They make safety credible.

## Safety Should Be Measurable

A good pact should produce artifacts.

Not just announcements.

Artifacts like:

- evaluation reports
- incident taxonomies
- model cards
- deployment criteria
- audit summaries
- misuse trend reports
- rollback policies
- safety-case documents
- external review notes

Some details may need redaction.

That is fine.

But there should be enough evidence for outsiders to tell whether the safety claim has substance.

If safety cannot be measured at all, it becomes branding.

If it can be measured only by the company making the claim, it becomes self-marking homework.

Neither is enough.

## The Takeaway

AI safety coordination is not automatically bad.

It may become necessary as systems get more powerful and more widely deployed.

But voluntary pacts among powerful companies need guardrails too.

The minimum credible package is:

```txt
public criteria
specific thresholds
independent audits
incident reporting
time limits
regulator visibility
open research access
competition safeguards
```

Safety needs coordination.

Competition needs protection.

Both can be true.

The answer is not secret handshakes.

The answer is clear rules.

Coordination needs sunlight.
