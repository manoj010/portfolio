# Anthropic's 5% Pledge Raises a Bigger Question: Who Decides?

Five percent is an easy number to remember.

It is also an incomplete description of Anthropic's new public-benefit fund.

On October 18, Anthropic announced that it plans to commit 5% of its shares to a new entity, subject to stockholder approval. The company says the stake could grow: its board may add another 2% for each 50% increase in the company's value above a baseline that has not yet been specified. Anthropic says the fund could support traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to particular populations, and that it will share more about the fund's governance in the coming months. ([Anthropic's announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund))

The announcement is a significant pledge. But the harder questions begin after the percentage: who will decide where the value goes, under what rules, and how will the public know whether the fund delivered what it promised?

## A Share Commitment Is Not The Same As A Cash Grant

The announcement is about company shares, not a fixed cash payment available today.

The value of those shares will move with the value of Anthropic. Turning a shareholding into money for grants or distributions would involve decisions about timing, liquidity, risk, and the fund's own rules. The announcement does not yet spell out those operating details.

Anthropic says the fund will be a wholly owned LLC rather than a 501(c)(3), because it wants flexibility between different forms of giving. It also says no equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving. Those are meaningful statements about the proposed structure and intended boundaries. They do not, by themselves, answer how the fund will select recipients, decide between different public needs, or report on outcomes.

The company also says that, combined with the founders' earlier equity pledges and its employee matching program, roughly 20% of Anthropic shares will be dedicated to the public good. That is Anthropic's description of several commitments together; it should not be mistaken for the new fund itself receiving 20% of the company.

## The Governing Rules Matter More Than The Tagline

“Public benefit” is a broad purpose, not an allocation formula.

Imagine the fund has to choose between supporting independent AI safety research, helping workers displaced by automation, funding biomedical work, or giving directly to people affected by technology. Each could plausibly serve the public. They are not interchangeable, and reasonable people will disagree about which should come first.

That makes governance central. A useful public-benefit vehicle needs clear answers to questions such as:

- Who appoints the people who make funding decisions?
- What conflicts of interest must they disclose or avoid?
- Can the company change the fund's priorities, or is there an independent decision process?
- How are grants or direct distributions selected and evaluated?
- How will the fund publish its finances, recipients, and results?
- What happens if the company's shares are difficult to sell or lose value?
- Can the fund's mission or structure change, and who approves that change?

These are not reasons to assume bad faith. They are the ordinary questions that turn an attractive commitment into an accountable institution.

## Independence Is A Design Choice To Explain

Anthropic presents the LLC structure as a way to be flexible, and flexibility may be useful if the fund wants to support different kinds of public benefit. But flexibility also makes transparent rules more important.

An LLC is a legal form. Its name alone does not tell us how independent the fund's decision-makers will be, what information they will publish, or how strongly its stated purpose will be protected. Anthropic says it will explain the structure and governance in the coming months. That information will matter more than guessing from the entity label.

This is a familiar tension in AI governance. Companies often need room to act quickly, but people outside the company need ways to understand and assess decisions that affect them. As I wrote in [Who Sets AI's Values?](/blog/who-sets-ai-values), declaring a set of values does not settle who gets to interpret them. Here the question is more concrete: who gets to define “public benefit” when real money and competing needs are involved?

## The Share Price Can Make The Headline Misleading

Because the fund's stake is in equity, its potential resources are tied to the company's future value. If Anthropic grows, a percentage of shares could be worth more. If the company faces setbacks or the shares cannot readily be sold, the fund's practical ability to distribute resources may look different from the headline percentage.

The step-up mechanism adds another layer. Anthropic says the board may add 2% for every 50% increase in company value above a baseline. “May” matters: the announced mechanism gives the board discretion, and the baseline has not yet been published. Readers should not treat the additions as automatic or calculate a present-day dollar value without those details.

The company says the fund may explore large donations to nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations. Those are possibilities, not a finalized grant program. The public should be able to tell the difference between an announced intention, an approved allocation, and a completed distribution.

## What Accountability Could Look Like

When the fuller structure is announced, a few practical features would make the commitment easier to evaluate:

1. **Published governance:** who makes decisions, how they are appointed, and how conflicts are handled.
2. **A clear funding policy:** whether the fund will hold, sell, or distribute shares, and how it handles liquidity and risk.
3. **Transparent allocations:** what was committed, what was actually paid, and to whom.
4. **Defined outcomes:** how the fund assesses whether its support helped, without pretending every social impact can be reduced to one metric.
5. **Independent scrutiny:** a process for financial audits and meaningful review of the fund's work.
6. **Change controls:** who can revise the mission, the rules, or the structure over time.

These are not claims that Anthropic has failed to provide them. The company has said that more details are forthcoming. They are a useful checklist for readers, employees, potential recipients, and other AI companies considering similar commitments.

## A Pledge Is A Beginning, Not A Verdict

There are two easy reactions to a corporate public-benefit announcement: treat the large number as proof of virtue, or dismiss it as public relations. Neither tells us much.

The more useful approach is to take the commitment seriously and ask how it will work. Anthropic has announced a 5% share commitment subject to shareholder approval, described a potential growth mechanism, and named some possible uses. It has also said the fund's governance details are still to come.

That leaves the most important part open. The fund's real public value will depend on its rules, decisions, transparency, and results—not only on the percentage in the first announcement.

When the governance plan arrives, the question will no longer be just “How much?” It will be “Who decides, who can challenge those decisions, and what evidence shows the public benefited?”
