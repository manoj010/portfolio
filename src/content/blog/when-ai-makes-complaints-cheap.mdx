# When AI Makes Complaints Cheap, Public Services Feel the Load

Writing a formal complaint takes work. You have to understand the process, find the right words, assemble the facts, and figure out what to ask for.

AI can make that part much easier. A short description of a missed collection, parking decision, workplace dispute, or information request can become a long, polished letter in minutes.

That is useful for people who struggle with forms, legal language, or confidence. It can also send a new kind of workload downstream: a public employee still has to understand the issue, check the claims, find the relevant record, and respond.

This tension was in the news on November 8, when *The Guardian* reported UK public bodies dealing with more AI-assisted correspondence. The story is a useful case study, but it needs to be read carefully: some of its evidence is first-hand reporting and practitioner testimony, not a national statistical audit proving that AI caused every increase in complaints or requests.

## Lowering The Cost Of Speaking Up

The people sending these requests are not all trying to overwhelm a system. AI can help someone state a problem clearly, understand a complicated process, or make an appeal they might otherwise abandon. In that sense, it can expand access to public processes that already reward people who know how to write formally.

That is the upside worth protecting. A request should not become less legitimate simply because its author used a writing assistant. A polished tone is not proof that a person is dishonest, and a clumsy or short message is not proof that the underlying concern is unimportant.

The capacity problem is real too. The Guardian reported local-government lawyers describing complaints that have grown much longer, and quoted an MP’s caseworker saying AI-assisted correspondence can take more time to process. Those are important signals from people doing the work, but they are not a representative measurement of every UK office.

For freedom-of-information requests, the Information Commissioner’s Office published guidance in May saying authorities were reporting higher volumes and more complex AI-generated requests, including some that misquote legislation or require clarification. The ICO’s guidance does not change the law: authorities should handle requests fairly and consistently regardless of how they were created.

## More Requests, Longer Requests, And Different Requests

“AI clog” can describe more than one operational change:

- **More submissions:** a tool can help people send requests they might otherwise not have made.
- **Longer submissions:** a simple concern may arrive wrapped in many paragraphs, citations, and side issues.
- **More complicated submissions:** generated text may mix several questions, assert legal rules from another jurisdiction, or include claims that need checking.
- **Faster follow-up:** a person can ask the model to draft another appeal or clarification immediately.

Those effects should not be collapsed into one headline number. The Guardian reported that Transport for London received a record 4,769 freedom-of-information requests in 2025–26, five percent above its previous peak in 2023–24. It also reported that TfL’s committee noted an increase in appeals that appeared AI-generated. The record volume is not itself proof that AI caused the increase; the reported AI-related observation concerns the appeals and should be treated separately.

Researchers have given this broader issue a name: **agentic flooding**. A preprint by Chris Schmitz, Lewis Hammond, and Alan Chan assembled 84 potential cases across 11 jurisdictions and discusses how cheaper AI-mediated requests may strain service capacity. The authors describe a dataset of reported cases, not a complete census or a controlled estimate of AI’s causal effect. That distinction matters: the work identifies a plausible pattern and policy problem, not a universal measured rate.

## The Wrong Fix Is To Make Access Harder For Everyone

When a queue gets longer, the quickest response can be to add friction: charge a fee, shorten forms, limit attachments, or block automated submissions. Those measures may reduce workload. They can also make it harder for people with disabilities, limited time, less formal education, language barriers, or weak access to advice to use a legitimate process.

The design goal should not be “stop people using AI.” It should be “make each request easier to understand and answer, without making the right to ask harder to exercise.”

Public bodies can start with the interface and the process:

- Ask for the specific decision, record, or outcome the person is seeking.
- Let people separate the main request from background or supporting material.
- Explain the jurisdiction and the process in plain language, with examples.
- Offer a way to clarify scope before a request turns into a long exchange.
- Track processing time, clarification rates, appeal outcomes, and access barriers, not just the raw number of submissions.

These are service-design improvements, not a reason to reject a request because it sounds machine-written. The ICO specifically advises authorities to apply existing FOI principles consistently regardless of how requests are created.

## Should The Institution Answer With AI Too?

It is tempting to place an AI summarizer at the other end of the inbox. It might group repeated points, extract the question being asked, or draft a first response. But if the organization uses AI to filter complaints, it can also hide a valid concern, flatten important context, or make the process harder to challenge.

The Australian Information Commissioner’s Office recently described the same tension in its complaint-handling work. It notes that AI may help triage and provide multilingual self-service, while emphasizing privacy, traceability, record-keeping, and human review. In its example of a significant FOI decision, staff may use a summary to support understanding, but still need to read the relevant documents themselves before deciding.

That is a sensible boundary. AI can help organize the queue; it should not silently decide whose request counts, which facts matter, or whether a person gets a response. People need to know when automation is involved and have a route to correct a summary or ask for human review.

## A Throughput Problem Is Also A Rights Problem

This is not just another example of AI producing more text than people can read. Public complaints and information requests are part of how people contest decisions and hold institutions accountable. If AI lowers the effort required to exercise those rights, the system needs to be ready for more participation. If it responds by making the process less accessible, the people who benefit least from AI assistance may lose out first.

The fair response is to improve how institutions receive, clarify, and process requests while measuring who is being helped or excluded. A long letter can be inefficient and still contain a valid claim. A short one can be decisive. The system should assess the substance and the applicable rules, not try to infer legitimacy from style.

I wrote about a related bottleneck in research, where [AI makes papers cheaper to produce while expert review remains scarce](/blog/when-ai-makes-papers-cheap-review-becomes-scarce). Public services face a different version of the challenge: keep the route to participation open while making the work on the receiving side manageable, accountable, and human-centered.
